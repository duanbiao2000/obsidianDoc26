### Part 3（3题，CRED融在表达里，不标框架）

**Q1: Is technology actually shrinking our attention spans?**
**示范：**
I'm pretty convinced it is, yeah. I mean, look at how we consume stuff now. Everything's optimized for speed—short-form videos under a minute, articles with bullet points every three lines, notifications that yank you out of whatever you're doing. I noticed it in myself recently: I was reading a novel—a novel, not some dense academic thing—and I caught myself checking my watch halfway through a chapter. That's genuinely concerning.

But here's what I'm not sure about: is it permanent? Like, are we rewiring our brains forever, or are we just training a bad habit? I suspect it's more the latter. We've basically conditioned ourselves to expect constant rewards. The problem is, that makes deep, linear thinking feel increasingly *uncomfortable*. And if you can't do that, you can't really solve anything complicated. So I don't think we're doomed or anything—but I do think we need to be more intentional about protecting our focus.

*（逻辑拆解：承认现象 → 个人例证 → 质疑绝对性 → 区分习惯vs永久损伤 → 点出深层后果）*

**Q2: Should schools ban smartphones on campus?**
**示范：**
Oof. A full ban? That feels like the nuclear option. I'd lean more toward restricted access, honestly.

My issue with an outright ban is that it avoids teaching the actual skill—which is self-regulation. If you just take phones away, kids never learn to handle temptation; they just learn to obey rules. And there's research showing that even having a phone face-down on a desk reduces cognitive performance, because it's the *awareness* of it that drains mental energy, not just the use.

But if we shield them completely until 18, they're going to hit college or the workplace with zero immunity to digital noise. That seems worse. So a better approach? Lockers during class, free during breaks. Let them practice compartmentalizing in a low-stakes environment. It's not perfect, but it acknowledges reality.

*（逻辑拆解：反对极端 → 提出核心矛盾 → 研究证据支撑 → 提出折中方案）*

**Q3: How might future tech change how we process information?**
**示范：**
I think we're heading toward something I'd call "curated passivity." Like, AI is getting scarily good at predicting what we want, so we don't have to search or evaluate anymore. Ten years ago, you typed a query and sifted through results. Now you just get one neat summary.

On the one hand, that's incredibly efficient. On the other hand, you're skipping the step where you actually *think*—cross-reference, doubt, make connections yourself. So we might get really fast at finding answers, but we might lose the ability to figure out what questions are worth asking. And that seems like a bigger loss than people realize. It's not about speed anymore; it's about whether we're even doing the cognitive work.

*（逻辑拆解：趋势预测 → 技术机制 → 对比过去与现在 → 揭示深层代价：丧失问题意识）*

**The Efficiency Paradox**

It's become a cultural given that digital tools make us more productive. But a growing body of evidence suggests that this convenience comes with a cognitive price. Researchers at the University of California tracked 500 knowledge workers over six months and found that those who routinely interrupted their core work to check emails took, on average, 23% longer to complete projects and produced solutions rated as less innovative by independent evaluators.

The mechanism appears to be the erosion of what psychologist Daniel Kahneman called "System 2" thinking—the slow, deliberate mode of reasoning required for complex problem-solving. Constant alerts keep the brain in "System 1"—fast, automatic, reactive. Over time, the brain becomes less capable of making the switch to deeper processing.

Critics point out that in many fast-paced work environments, real-time responsiveness is non-negotiable. And they're not wrong. But the same researchers replicated their findings in an actual office setting—not just a lab—and the effect persisted. The real takeaway isn't to abandon digital tools; it's to carve out protected "deep work" blocks. The most effective practitioners, they found, were those who used tools like focus timers to enforce uninterrupted periods, not those who tried to multitask.

**True / False / Not Given:**

1. According to the researchers, taking email checks during core work resulted in longer project completion times.
2. The researchers found that real-time responsiveness was essential in all office environments.
3. The study's findings were only observed in controlled laboratory conditions.

**Answers with logic:**

1. **TRUE** – *"took, on average, 23% longer to complete projects"* directly matches. No deflection.
2. **FALSE** – *"Critics point out that... real-time responsiveness is non-negotiable"* – this is the *critics'* claim, not the researchers'. The question says "the researchers found," which is unsubstantiated. Wait, actually: does the text say the researchers found *the opposite*? They merely note that critics say responsiveness is non-negotiable, but never endorse it. So "essential in all offices" is an absolutist claim not made by the researchers. This is a subtle one: it's **NOT GIVEN** because the text only mentions the critics' view, not the researchers' position on universality.
3. **FALSE** – *"the same researchers replicated their findings in an actual office setting—not just a lab"* – directly contradicts the statement.
