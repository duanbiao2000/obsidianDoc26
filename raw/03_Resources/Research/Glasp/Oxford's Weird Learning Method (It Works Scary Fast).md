## Summary

- **[00:00] Mastering a subject in 7 days:** Oxford students learn quickly by focusing on questions and defending ideas face-to-face — not by traditional slow coverage.  
- **[00:22] Coverage ≠ understanding:** Most people follow a syllabus and try to cover everything, but true understanding comes from targeted, question-driven learning.  
- **[01:06] Learning against a deadline:** Oxford uses weekly questions and essays, which are not graded, encouraging students to take risks and learn from mistakes without fear of failure.  
- **[01:49] The importance of questioning:** Students pick a question, write an argument, and then defend or attack it, fostering deep engagement and critical thinking.  
- **[02:38] Defining key words:** Before answering, students define important terms in their own words to clarify assumptions and sharpen their understanding.  
- **[03:00] Analyzing questions deeply:** Instead of quick answers, Oxford students dissect questions to uncover hidden assumptions and control the answer’s direction.  
- **[04:33] Words reveal understanding:** Defining words exposes whether you truly understand concepts, preventing superficial knowledge and vague arguments.  
- **[05:43] Focused reading on positions:** Instead of reading entire books, students identify the core claims and contradictions, making reading efficient and purposeful.  
- **[06:56] Judging and arguing:** Students evaluate conflicting sources, decide which is stronger, and then argue the opposite side to deepen comprehension and critical judgment.  
- **[08:27] Building skills through argumentation:** The method develops understanding, judgment, and clarity quickly, but true skills like speaking or playing require hands-on practice outside of arguments.

Every week, the student gets one question, not a topic, a question.

The question is chosen before any reading happens. Read that again. The question comes first. The reading comes second. Why does the order matter? Because a question turns your brain into a hunting brain.
- [01:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=69s) When you know what you're looking for, every page either matters or it doesn't. You stop absorbing. You start searching. Around 2,000 words that take a position and defend it.
- [01:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=86s) Then comes the room. The student sits across from a tutor, often one of the top living experts on that exact question. The real exams come at the very end in the final weeks of the final year, and those decide the entire degree. So, for 3 years, the student can be wrong every single week for free.

Being wrong costs nothing, so students take real positions instead of safe ones, and a real position that gets destroyed on Thursday teaches more than a month of note-taking. - [02:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=144s) Pick one question on Monday before you read anything. - [02:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=165s) The key is this, a question with a deadline creates learning. A syllabus with a schedule creates coverage. Pick the question first and make an argument the finish line, not notes. That's the first method. But there's a trap inside it. Most people grab the question and start answering right away, and that's exactly how you build a confident, well-written, completely empty argument.
- [03:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=184s) Oxford has a fix for that, too. Here's what schools taught you, read the question, answer the question. Fast answers look smart, first hand up wins. Oxford students do the opposite, they spend the first hour attacking the question itself. Take a question like, was Napoleon a great leader? - [03:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=206s) An Oxford student stops at the second word, great. What does great mean here? Effective, moral, historically important? Because Napoleon is a different answer under each definition. Effective? Yes. Moral? Good luck with that one. Then the next word, leader. Leader of what? The army, the state, an idea? Every load-bearing word gets defined by you, in your own words, before a single fact enters the essay.
- [03:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=230s) That sounds like stalling, it's the opposite. Here's why. Every question hides an assumption. Was Napoleon a great leader? Assumes greatness is one single thing that leaders either have or don't. Maybe that's false. Maybe he was great at war and a disaster at peace and the question is broken. Surface the hidden assumption and you find the real question underneath.
- [04:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=250s) The person who defines the words controls the answer. In a tutorial, in a debate, in a boardroom, every time. And here's the uncomfortable part. Undefined language is where fake understanding lives. You can talk for 10 minutes about the economy or consciousness or innovation and not survive one follow-up question because you never defined the word.
- [04:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=269s) You borrowed it. It sounds like knowledge right up until someone asks you what you mean. - [04:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=287s) Your version. Take the question and pick the three words doing the most work. Define each one in your own words on paper. - [05:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=358s) Stop reading for completion. Start reading for positions. Here's how it works. Every serious author is arguing something. - [06:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=376s) What is this author actually saying? Find it. Write it down in one sentence. Leave. - [06:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1JIW6b4n-is&t=396s) Author A says the Industrial Revolution lifted workers up. Author B says it crushed them.
