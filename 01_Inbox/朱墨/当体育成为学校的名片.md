#### The Elephant in the Quad

At Tufts University, a forty-foot bronze elephant stands near the center of campus, its trunk raised in a gesture that students have long interpreted as good luck. The statue commemorates Jumbo—not merely a mascot in the abstract sense, but a real elephant that once belonged to P.T. Barnum's circus and became the university's symbol after Barnum, an original trustee of Tufts College, donated Jumbo's stuffed hide to the institution in 1889. The original Jumbo remained on display until a fire destroyed it in 1975. Today, the statue serves as a reminder of a tradition that is both whimsical and deeply embedded in the university's identity.

Tufts is not alone in having a distinctive mascot, but few universities can claim that their symbol was once the most famous animal in the world. The story of Jumbo—captured as a calf in Africa, sold to the London Zoo, then purchased by Barnum for $10,000, and ultimately killed in a railway accident while reportedly trying to save a baby elephant—has become part of the university's lore. But the question that lingers is whether this outsized symbol reflects something deeper about the institution's values, particularly as Tufts has emerged as a competitive force in NCAA Division III athletics. [^culture:NCAA DIII]

Tufts fields 28 varsity sports and competes in the New England Small College Athletic Conference (NESCAC). Since 2010, its varsity teams have won 14 NCAA championships and 53 NESCAC championships. On paper, this is a record of impressive athletic achievement. Yet Tufts is also a nationally ranked university—tied for 36th among National Universities in the 2026 U.S. News rankings, with an overall score of 80 out of 100. It is, by any measure, a serious academic institution. The question, then, is whether these two identities coexist comfortably or whether they represent a tension that is endemic to elite American higher education.

Critics of the growing prominence of college athletics argue that sports, particularly at the Division I level, have become a distraction from the core mission of universities. They point to scandals involving academic fraud, the exploitation of student-athletes, and the disproportionate allocation of resources to athletic facilities. Tufts, however, operates in Division III, a tier defined by the principle that "student athletes' academic and community involvement mirror that of the general student body". There are no athletic scholarships at this level, and the emphasis is explicitly on participation rather than professionalization.

The Division III model, its defenders argue, offers a resolution to the tension between academics and athletics. At Tufts, the term "student-athlete" is reportedly taken seriously, with coaches and players embracing a "dual identity". The academic success of student-athletes has been consistent for decades; in a recent year, Tufts student-athletes achieved a cumulative grade point average of 3.375 across all sports. This suggests that, at least in this institutional context, athletic participation does not necessarily come at the expense of academic rigour.

Yet the symbolic weight of Jumbo—and the athletic success the mascot represents—raises a different question. When a university invests heavily in its athletic identity, does it risk cultivating a culture that is more "grounded" or more "anti-intellectual"? The answer is not straightforward. On one hand, a visible athletic programme can make an elite institution feel more accessible, more connected to the broader public, less insulated from the concerns of ordinary life. The Jumbo statue, after all, is not a monument to an obscure scholar but to an elephant—an animal that children recognise and adults remember from childhood stories. There is something deliberately unpretentious about this choice.

On the other hand, the elevation of sports to a central place in institutional identity can also signal a shift in priorities. When the most visible symbols of a university are athletic rather than academic, when students are more likely to recognise the football coach than the Nobel laureate on the faculty, the institution's public identity becomes shaped by forces that have little to do with its intellectual mission. This is not a problem unique to Tufts, but it is a problem that any university with a strong athletic culture must confront.

Perhaps the most instructive observation comes from within the Tufts community itself. One admissions essay noted that students "are famously self-aware about their university's identity". This self-awareness—the capacity to recognise the tension and still choose the institution—may be the most important factor. A university that can sustain a quirky elephant mascot, a competitive Division III athletic programme, and a top-40 academic ranking without pretending that these elements are entirely compatible is, in some sense, modelling the kind of nuanced thinking that higher education is supposed to cultivate.

I want to talk about something a bit unusual—the mascot of Tufts University in Massachusetts, which is an elephant named Jumbo. And I think it's a surprisingly good representation of what the university is about.

#### Part 3 示范

**Q1: Why do you think some universities invest heavily in their sports programmes?**
- **示范回答：** The most obvious answer is visibility. In an increasingly crowded higher education market, a successful sports team is one of the most effective ways to get your institution's name into public consciousness. It's **asymmetric** /ˌeɪsɪˈmetrɪk/ marketing—a single football game can reach millions of people in a way that a research breakthrough, however significant, rarely does. But I think there's a deeper driver as well, which is about institutional identity and community building. Universities are not just collections of departments and research centres; they're communities of people who need to feel connected to something larger than themselves. Sports provide a **focal point** /ˈfoʊkəl pɔɪnt/ for that connection—a shared experience that transcends disciplinary boundaries. The question, of course, is whether that connection comes at the expense of other forms of community, particularly intellectual ones. [^logic:asymmetric] [^culture:community building]

**Q2: Some people argue that elite universities should focus on academics rather than sports. Do you agree?**
- **示范回答：** I think the framing of the question is slightly misleading, because it assumes a zero-sum relationship—that resources devoted to sports are necessarily resources taken away from academics. That's not always how institutional budgets work; athletic programmes can generate revenue, alumni engagement, and visibility that indirectly benefit the academic mission. That said, I do think there's a legitimate concern about **mission creep** /ˈmɪʃən kriːp/. When a university's most visible symbols are athletic rather than intellectual, when the president spends more time at football games than at faculty seminars, the institution's public identity shifts in ways that are difficult to reverse. The danger isn't that sports exist; it's that they become the *primary* story the institution tells about itself. And that, I think, is a problem—not because sports are bad, but because they're not what universities are *for*. [^writing:mission creep]

**Q3: Do you think a strong sports culture can change the identity of a university?**
- **示范回答：** I think it already has, for many institutions. The transformation of American universities from relatively modest regional colleges to national brands has been driven, in part, by the visibility that sports provide. A university that wins a championship becomes known not just for its academic programmes but for its athletic identity—and that identity can **persist** /pərˈsɪst/ long after the specific victories are forgotten. But I'd want to distinguish between two kinds of change. There's the superficial change—the perception shift, the brand awareness, the recognition factor. And then there's the deeper change, which is about what the institution actually values, how it allocates resources, what kinds of behaviour it rewards. The former can happen quite quickly; the latter takes much longer and is much harder to reverse. A university can acquire a reputation as a "sports school" without fundamentally changing its academic culture. But if the sports culture starts to shape hiring decisions, promotion criteria, or resource allocation, then the identity change is real and potentially **consequential** /ˌkɑːnsɪˈkwenʃəl/. [^writing:perception vs reality]

#### 录音文本

"Good afternoon. I'm Michael Chen, and I teach the history of American higher education at a university in the Northeast.

Today I want to talk about something that might seem trivial at first glance: university mascots. But I'd argue that mascots tell us a great deal about institutional identity—about how universities want to be seen, and about the tensions they navigate between different aspects of their mission.

Let me use a specific example: Tufts University in Massachusetts. Tufts' mascot is Jumbo the Elephant—and Jumbo was not a cartoon character. He was a real elephant, one of the most famous animals of the 19th century. He was owned by P.T. Barnum, the circus **impresario** /ˌɪmprəˈsɑːrioʊ/, who happened to be an original trustee of Tufts College. When Jumbo died in a railway accident in Canada in 1885, Barnum donated his stuffed hide to the university. For nearly ninety years, the actual Jumbo—or at least his remains—stood in a museum on campus, until a fire destroyed him in 1975. Today, a bronze statue commemorates him.

Now, you might ask: why does any of this matter? I think it matters because the choice of mascot reflects something about how an institution sees itself. Tufts could have chosen something **dignified** /ˈdɪɡnɪfaɪd/—a lion, an eagle, something that conveys gravitas. Instead, they chose an elephant from a circus. That choice is **deliberately** /dɪˈlɪbərətli/ unpretentious. It says: we don't take ourselves too seriously.

But there's another dimension to this story. In recent decades, Tufts has also developed a strong athletic programme. They compete in NCAA Division III, which is the division that explicitly prioritises the academic experience of student-athletes. Since 2010, Tufts has won 14 NCAA championships and 53 conference championships. So they're competitive—very competitive—but within a framework that insists that academics come first.

This, I think, is the deeper story that the Jumbo mascot tells. Tufts is saying: we can be both. We can have a quirky, playful identity and still be a serious academic institution. We can compete and win in sports without losing sight of why we exist as a university. Whether that balance is sustainable—whether it can survive the pressures of commercialisation and the relentless demand for visibility—that's a question for another talk." ### 写作示范与结构

#### 示范段落

**Opening Paragraph:**
The debate over whether universities should prioritise athletics or academics is often framed as a choice between mutually exclusive goods—as if resources devoted to one are necessarily stolen from the other. This framing, however, obscures a more interesting question: not whether sports and scholarship can coexist, but what kind of institutional identity emerges when they do. My contention is not that athletic investment is inherently problematic, but that its symbolic weight—the stories a university tells about itself through its sports programmes—can shape its culture in ways that are difficult to anticipate and harder to reverse. [^writing:mutually exclusive]

**Body Paragraph 1:**
The case for athletic investment rests on a logic that is both intuitive and empirically defensible. A successful sports team generates visibility, alumni engagement, and a sense of collective identity that transcends the fragmentation of academic disciplines. For institutions competing in an increasingly crowded higher education market, these are not trivial benefits. Yet the logic becomes precarious when visibility is mistaken for substance, when the metrics of athletic success—wins, championships, rankings—begin to displace the slower, less televisible work of intellectual inquiry. The danger is not that universities will stop doing research or teaching; it is that the public identity of the institution will become so thoroughly associated with its athletic performance that its academic mission becomes secondary, not by design but by **attrition** /əˈtrɪʃən/. The question, then, is not whether sports have value, but whether their value can be contained within a framework that keeps the academic mission primary. [^writing:by attrition]
