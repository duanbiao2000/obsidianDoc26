# Town and Gown

**Town and gown** refers to the historical tension between the local townspeople (the "town") and the members of the [[University of Oxford]] (the "gown", referring to academic dress). This conflict was a driving force in Oxford's institutional development.

## Origins

As the university grew after 1167, the influx of students and scholars created friction with the existing population. Disputes over prices, privileges, and jurisdiction were common. The university sought self-governance and exemption from local laws, while townspeople resented the students' perceived arrogance and legal immunity.

## Key Events

- **c. 1205**: Two students were lynched by Oxford locals. In response, a group of scholars fled to Cambridge, founding the [[University of Cambridge]].
- **St. Scholastica Day Riot (1355)**: A three-day riot that began with a dispute over wine quality and escalated into mass violence, leaving 93 people dead. It became the most notorious flashpoint of town-gown conflict.
- **Later history**: Tensions gradually institutionalised. Today, students make up over 25% of Oxford's population, and the relationship is largely peaceful, though the historical divide remains part of local culture.

## Consequences

- The college system was a direct response: independent halls of residence (later colleges) allowed students to live separately from townspeople.
- The founding of Cambridge University was an unintended consequence of town-gown violence.
- The term "town and gown" is now used generically to describe similar university-community tensions worldwide.
