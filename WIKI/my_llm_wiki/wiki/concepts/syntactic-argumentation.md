# Syntactic Argumentation

Syntactic argumentation is the principle that grammatical structure itself carries argumentative weight, independent of lexical content. Rather than relying on explicit reasoning or evidence, the speaker or writer uses syntactic patterns to pre-commit the listener to a conclusion. The "shape" of a sentence — its grammatical architecture — does the persuasive work before the content is fully processed.

## Core Techniques

The framework identifies five primary syntactic techniques, plus a rhythmic device:

1. **[[concessive-reversal]]** — Concedes a point before negating it, placing the opposing view in a subordinate position
2. **[[parallel-contrast]]** — Two sentences with identical syntactic shells but contrasting lexical content, letting contrast do the judging
3. **[[dangling-participle]]** — A fronted participial phrase that sets emotional tone for the main clause
4. **[[subjunctive-as-argumentation]]** — Counterfactual constructions that define essence by negation
5. **[[nominalization-chain-reaction]]** — Compressing causal arguments into noun phrase subjects, packaging opinion as established fact
6. **[[short-sentence-hammer]]** — A brief, declarative sentence following a long, complex one, creating rhythmic rupture and finality

## Pedagogical Claim

The source asserts that high-scoring English speakers (IELTS 8.5) argue primarily through syntactic structure, while band 7 candidates rely on content. This claim is presented as pedagogical shorthand rather than empirical finding.
