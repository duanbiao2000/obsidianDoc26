# Excessive Nominalization

**Excessive Nominalization** is the stylistic error of converting strong, dynamic verbs into abstract nouns, which are then paired with weak, generic verbs (such as *make*, *give*, *have*, or *take*). This practice leads to wordy, passive, and less impactful sentences.

## Mechanism

The pattern typically follows: **Weak Verb + Abstract Noun**.
*   *Example*: "We **made a decision**" instead of "We **decided**."
*   *Example*: "He **gave an explanation**" instead of "He **explained**."

## Negative Impacts

1.  **Reduced Conciseness**: Increases word count without adding information.
2.  **Loss of Agency**: Shifts focus away from the actor and the action.
3.  **Passive Tone**: Creates a static, bureaucratic feel rather than an active, engaging one.
4.  **Cognitive Load**: Forces the reader to unpack the noun phrase to understand the core action.

## Correction Strategy

To correct excessive nominalization, identify the core action within the noun phrase and convert it back into a strong verb.
*   *Have a discussion* → **Discuss**
*   *Take into consideration* → **Consider**
*   *Come to a conclusion* → **Conclude**
*   *Make an assumption* → **Assume**

## Contextual Note

While some nominalization is necessary in academic writing for cohesion and abstraction, *excessive* use—particularly when driven by generic verbs—is a hallmark of poor style. It is distinct from necessary nominalization used for thematic progression or technical precision.
