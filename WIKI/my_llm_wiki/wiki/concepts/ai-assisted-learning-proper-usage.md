# AI-Assisted Learning: Proper vs. Improper Usage

This concept defines the boundary between using AI as a **thinking amplifier** (proper) versus a **thinking replacement** (improper) in the context of writing and learning.

## What AI Can Help With

- **Grammar correction**: Fixing surface-level errors.
- **Vocabulary substitution**: Suggesting more precise or varied word choices (after the writer's thinking is clear).
- **Structure optimization**: Improving paragraph organization and flow.

## What AI Cannot Help With

- **Originality of ideas**: AI cannot generate genuinely novel insights for the writer.
- **Rigor of reasoning**: AI cannot ensure the logical soundness of an argument.
- **Relevance of examples**: AI cannot guarantee that examples are contextually appropriate.
- **Response to counterarguments**: AI cannot anticipate and address opposing views with the writer's own reasoning.

## Recommended Workflow

1. **Write the full draft yourself** — trains generative thinking.
2. **Revise it yourself** — trains metacognitive monitoring.
3. **Ask AI to analyze** logical gaps and unclear expressions.
4. **Judge AI's analysis** — decide what to accept, reject, or modify.

## Common Pitfalls

- Having AI expand an outline into a full essay (trains zero cognitive skills).
- Using AI's rewrite without understanding why it is better.
- Relying on AI for idea generation rather than using it as a refinement tool.

## Tension with Automation

This concept creates a productive tension with the wiki's [[automation]] concept (from data engineering). While operational automation (e.g., data pipelines) improves efficiency through tool delegation, cognitive automation (having AI write for you) undermines learning. The key distinction is **domain**: operational tasks vs. cognitive development tasks.
