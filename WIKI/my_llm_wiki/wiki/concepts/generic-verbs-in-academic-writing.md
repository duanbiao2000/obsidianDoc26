# Generic Verbs in Academic Writing

**Generic Verbs** refer to high-frequency, low-information verbs (such as *get*, *make*, *do*, *have*, *take*) that serve as grammatical placeholders but fail to convey precise meaning. In academic and professional writing, their overuse reduces **information density** and undermines the writer's authority.

## Characteristics

1.  **Low Information Density**: They do not specify the manner, intensity, or nature of the action. For example, *get* could mean *download*, *retrieve*, *derive*, or *purchase*.
2.  **Ambiguity**: They force the reader to infer the specific action from context, increasing cognitive load.
3.  **Informality**: They are often associated with spoken English or beginner-level proficiency, making them unsuitable for formal registers.

## Impact on Writing Quality

*   **Precision**: Replacing *do a study* with *conduct a study* clarifies the methodological rigor.
*   **Conciseness**: Avoiding nominalizations like *make a decision* in favor of *decide* streamlines sentence structure.
*   **Professional Tone**: Using substantive verbs like *formulate* instead of *make* (for plans) signals expertise.

## Strategies for Improvement

*   **Verb Specificity Strategy**: Ask "What is the specific action?" to identify the most accurate verb.
*   **Avoid Nominalization Traps**: Convert "weak verb + abstract noun" structures back into strong single verbs (e.g., *give a suggestion* → *suggest*).
*   **Limit Phrasal Verbs**: Replace informal phrasal verbs (*talk about*) with formal equivalents (*discuss*) in written contexts.
