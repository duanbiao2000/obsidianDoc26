# College System (Oxford)

The **college system** is the distinctive organisational model of the [[University of Oxford]], where the university is composed of **39 self-governing colleges**, each functioning as a mini-institution within the larger framework.

## Origins

The college system emerged from the "town and gown" tensions of medieval Oxford. As the university grew after 1167, conflicts with local townspeople prompted the construction of independent halls of residence where students could live separately. These halls evolved into colleges.

The template for the modern college system was set by **[[Merton College]]** (founded 1264). Its founder, **Walter de Merton**, wrote a set of statutes that transformed a simple dormitory into a self-governing community with its own governance, property, and rules. This model was adopted by all subsequent colleges.

## Characteristics

- **Self-governance**: Each college has its own head, governing body, statutes, and coat of arms.
- **Identity**: Students identify strongly with their college; colleges have distinct traditions and rivalries.
- **Accommodation and teaching**: Colleges provide housing, dining, pastoral care, and small-group teaching (tutorials), while the university sets examinations and awards degrees.
- **Geographic grouping (historical)**: Students were originally grouped by geographic origin (north vs. south England), though this practice faded.

## Consequences

- The college system reduced town-gown conflict by providing controlled student housing.
- It created a decentralised, resilient structure that allowed Oxford to grow organically.
- The same model was adopted by the [[University of Cambridge]], which was founded by Oxford scholars fleeing violence (~1205).

## Notable Colleges

- [[Christ Church College]] — largest, founded 1546, 13 UK Prime Ministers.
- [[Merton College]] — first with written statutes.
- [[Magdalen College]] — May Morning tradition.
- [[All Souls College]] — research-only, no undergraduates.
