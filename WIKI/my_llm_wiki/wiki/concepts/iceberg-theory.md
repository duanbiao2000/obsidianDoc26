# Iceberg Theory

The **Iceberg Theory** (also known as the **Theory of Omission**) is a literary technique pioneered by [[Ernest Hemingway]]. It posits that the deeper meaning of a story should not be evident on the surface but should shine through implicitly.

## Principles

*   **Omission:** The author omits explicit explanations of characters' thoughts, feelings, or motivations.
*   **Surface Detail:** Only the "tip of the iceberg"—actions, dialogue, and physical descriptions—is presented.
*   **Reader Inference:** The reader must infer the underlying emotional and thematic weight from these surface details.

## Application

In *For Whom the Bell Tolls*, Hemingway uses this theory to depict the protagonist's acceptance of death. Instead of internal monologues about fear or bravery, the text describes Jordan touching the grass and checking his gun. This restraint creates a powerful sense of dignity and allows the reader to project their own understanding of the character's state of mind.

## Relation to Other Concepts

*   **[[Show-dont-tell]]:** The Iceberg Theory is a specific, rigorous application of the broader "show, don't tell" principle.
*   **[[British-academic-style]]:** While distinct in origin, the analytical appreciation of omission and subtext aligns with high-level academic literary criticism.
