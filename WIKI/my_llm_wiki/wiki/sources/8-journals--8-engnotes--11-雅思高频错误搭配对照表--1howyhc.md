## Key Concepts
- **Collocation Errors**: The primary focus of the source. These are errors where words are combined in ways that are not standard or natural in English, even if grammatically correct. The source categorizes these into Verb+Noun, Adjective+Noun, Noun+Verb, Prepositional, and Adverb+Adjective.
- **Register/Formality Shifts**: The source emphasizes moving from informal/incorrect phrasing (e.g., "do a crime") to formal/academic equivalents (e.g., "commit a crime") suitable for IELTS writing and speaking.
- **Extreme Adjectives**: A specific grammatical concept highlighted in the "Degree Adverbs" section, explaining why intensifiers like "very" are incorrect with absolute adjectives like "unique" or "perfect."
- **Subject-Verb Agreement in Abstract Nouns**: The source addresses how abstract nouns (economy, population, crime) should be paired with specific verbs (improves, grows, rises) rather than generic ones (becomes better).

## Main Arguments & Findings
- **Core Claim**: Chinese learners of English frequently make systematic collocation errors due to direct translation or overgeneralization of simple verbs (like "do" or "make"). Correcting these is more impactful for IELTS scores than fixing basic grammar.
- **Evidence**: The document provides extensive tables contrasting common errors (❌) with correct forms (✅) across seven categories:
    - **Crime/Law**: "Commit" is required for crimes; "violate/break" for laws.
    - **Research**: "Conduct/carry out" is used for research/surveys/experiments; "research" is uncountable.
    - **Problems**: "Address/tackle/alleviate" are preferred over "solve" for complex issues like pollution or poverty.
    - **Economy**: "Boost/stimulate/foster" are better than "raise/develop" for economic growth.
    - **Prepositions**: Specific prepositions are mandatory (e.g., "influence on," "role in," "invest in").
    - **Adjectives**: "High" goes with speed/pressure; "heavy" with rain/traffic; "strong" with wind/argument.
    - **Fixed Phrases**: "Make progress," "do homework," "give advice," "cause damage."
- **Strength of Evidence**: High practical utility. The distinctions align with standard academic English usage guides and IELTS band descriptors which reward lexical resource precision.
