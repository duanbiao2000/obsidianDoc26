# Eliminate Weak Language: IELTS Band 7 vs. Band 9 Strategies

This source applies the leadership communication principle of "Eliminating Weak Language" to the context of **IELTS** preparation. It argues that weak vocabulary is not merely a stylistic flaw but a reflection of undisciplined thinking, which directly impacts scoring in **Lexical Resource**, **Fluency & Coherence**, and **Task Response**.

## Core Argument

The central thesis is that **weak language** (vague, hesitant, or apologetic phrasing) signals a lack of confidence and clarity to examiners. To achieve **Band 9**, candidates must replace these with strong, precise, and assertive language that demonstrates clear stance and logical density.

## Key Concepts of Weak Language

### 1. Lexical Hedging
Excessive use of modifiers like *quite*, *somewhat*, *maybe*, or *I think* reduces precision. In academic contexts, this is often interpreted as uncertainty rather than caution.

- **Weak**: "I think pollution is quite bad."
- **Strong**: "Pollution is one of the defining crises of this century."

### 2. Circular Opening
Starting an essay or response by merely restating the prompt without adding a unique perspective or stance. This is identified as a common **Band 7** trap.

- **Weak**: "This essay will discuss whether social media has positive or negative effects on society."
- **Strong**: "Social media has fractured public discourse more profoundly than any technology since the printing press — yet the mechanism is poorly understood."

### 3. Filler Transitionals
Using generic connectors (*also*, *furthermore*, *in conclusion*) without logical progression. Strong transitions drive the argument forward.

- **Weak**: "Also, furthermore, additionally..."
- **Strong**: "More critically / What follows from this is / The implications extend further."

### 4. Vague Quantifiers
Using non-specific amounts (*many*, *a lot*, *some*) instead of precise data or structured descriptions.

- **Weak**: "Many people believe that a lot of countries have some kind of problem with inequality."
- **Strong**: "Wealth concentration has intensified across OECD nations over the past four decades, leaving the bottom quintile structurally excluded from economic recovery."

### 5. Apologetic Register
Language that preemptively excuses the speaker’s uncertainty, particularly detrimental in **IELTS Speaking Part 3**.

- **Weak**: "I'm not really sure but maybe..."
- **Strong**: "The evidence is mixed, but the stronger case is..."

## Psychological Shift: Certainty vs. Hesitation

The source emphasizes that high scores require a shift from seeking validation ("Does that make sense?") to asserting expertise. Candidates are encouraged to adopt a mindset where every word carries weight, avoiding the use of word count to mask low思维 density (thinking density).

## Practical Exercise: The 24-Hour Correction

Candidates are advised to catch every weak phrase mid-sentence, stop, correct it, and say it stronger. This builds the habit of **precision** and **assertiveness**.

## Diagnostic Questions

After writing or speaking a sentence, ask:
1. If I remove this sentence, does the argument weaken? (If no, it's filler.)
2. Can this word be replaced with something more precise? (If yes, it's weak.)
3. Am I expressing a stance or avoiding it? (Avoidance is weak.)
