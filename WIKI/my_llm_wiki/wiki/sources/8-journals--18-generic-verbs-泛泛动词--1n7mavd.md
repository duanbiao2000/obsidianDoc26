# Generic Verbs 泛泛动词

This source addresses a critical linguistic pitfall for ESL (English as a Second Language) learners: the overuse of **Generic Verbs** (泛泛动词). These are high-frequency, low-information verbs such as *get*, *make*, *do*, *have*, and *take* that act as grammatical placeholders. While they ensure grammatical correctness, they significantly reduce **information density**, clarity, and professional tone by failing to convey the specific manner, intensity, or nature of an action.

## Core Problem: Information Density Reduction

The central argument is that relying on these "universal words" forces the reader or listener to guess the specific meaning of the action. This leads to:
*   **Decreased Accuracy**: Ambiguity regarding how an action was performed.
*   **Reduced Clarity**: Lack of vividness and precision.
*   **Mediocre Style**: A perception of beginner-level proficiency rather than refined expression.

## Key Verb Categories and Substantive Alternatives

The source provides detailed comparisons for five primary generic verbs, offering **Substantive Verbs** that enhance precision:

### 1. Get
Often misused for acquisition, change of state, or achievement.
*   *get a result* → **obtain / achieve / derive**
*   *get information* → **acquire / retrieve / gather**
*   *get angry* → **become / grow / turn**

### 2. Make
Used lazily to indicate creation or causation without specifying the process.
*   *make a decision* → **reach / render / arrive at**
*   *make a plan* → **formulate / devise / construct**
*   *make sure* → **ensure / verify / guarantee**

### 3. Do
A vague filler verb lacking specific imagery.
*   *do a study* → **conduct / carry out / perform**
*   *do homework* → **complete / execute**
*   *do business* → **transact**

### 4. Have
Passive construction that masks active agency.
*   *have a meeting* → **hold / convene / conduct**
*   *have a look* → **examine / inspect / review**
*   *have a problem* → **encounter / face**

### 5. Take
Often part of redundant phrasal structures.
*   *take into consideration* → **consider**
*   *take place* → **occur / unfold**
*   *take time* → **require / demand**

## Secondary Pitfalls: Nominalization and Phrasal Verbs

Beyond single verbs, the source identifies two related stylistic errors:

### Excessive Nominalization
Converting strong verbs into abstract nouns paired with weak verbs (e.g., *give a suggestion* instead of *suggest*). This creates wordy, passive sentences.
*   *make a decision* → **decide**
*   *have a discussion* → **discuss**
*   *come to a conclusion* → **conclude**

### Reliance on Phrasal Verbs
Overusing informal verb+preposition combinations (*talk about*, *look for*) in formal contexts.
*   *talk about* → **discuss / address**
*   *look for* → **seek / explore**
*   *find out* → **determine / ascertain**

## Nuance: Prepositional Precision

The source highlights how slight prepositional changes alter logical focus, particularly with *Decide* and *Agree*:
*   **Decide**: Committing to an action (*decided to study*).
*   **Decide on**: Selecting from options (*decided on Paris*).
*   **Agree with**: Aligning with a person's view.
*   **Agree to**: Accepting a proposal or term.

## Strategic Application

The recommended strategy is the **Verb Specificity Strategy**: when using a generic verb, ask "What is the specific action?" and replace it with a substantive alternative that captures the method or intent. This is crucial for **IELTS Writing** and academic/professional communication where precision signals higher proficiency.
