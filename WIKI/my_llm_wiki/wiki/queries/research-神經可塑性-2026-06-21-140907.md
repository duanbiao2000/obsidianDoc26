# Research: 神經可塑性

# Neuroplasticity

**Neuroplasticity**, also known as neural plasticity or brain plasticity, refers to the nervous system's capacity to adaptively change its structure, functions, and connections in response to intrinsic or extrinsic stimuli [1][7][10]. This dynamic biological process enables the brain to reorganize neural pathways throughout an individual's lifespan, facilitating learning, memory consolidation, skill acquisition, sensory adaptation, and recovery from neurological injuries [2][8].

## Historical Development
The conceptualization of neuroplasticity evolved from early philosophical observations to rigorous empirical validation. In 1793, Italian anatomist Michele Vincenzo Malacarne observed that extensively trained animals exhibited substantially larger cerebellums than untrained counterparts, though these findings were largely forgotten [1][8]. In 1890, William James introduced the term "plasticity" to behavior in *The Principles of Psychology*, proposing that the brain and its functions are not fixed throughout adulthood [1][8]. Despite this early insight, the early 20th-century scientific consensus, heavily influenced by Santiago Ramón y Cajal's later assertions that adult nerve paths are "fixed, ended, immutable," held that the mature brain lacked regenerative or adaptive capacity [4][8].

The paradigm shifted decisively following mid-20th-century research. Jerzy Konorski popularized the term "neural plasticity," and Donald Hebb's 1949 work on associative learning established the theoretical groundwork for experience-dependent synaptic modification [7][8]. Empirical validation emerged in 1964 when Marian Diamond and colleagues demonstrated anatomical brain plasticity in rats exposed to enriched environments [1][8]. Subsequent breakthroughs by Michael Merzenich, Jon Kaas, Paul Bach-y-Rita, and Eleanor Maguire (whose landmark studies on London taxi drivers revealed hippocampal structural changes correlated with navigational expertise) conclusively proved that experience-dependent reorganization persists into adulthood and old age [1][8].

## Core Mechanisms and Classifications
While researchers acknowledge the lack of a universally agreed-upon theoretical framework, neuroplasticity is broadly categorized into structural and functional domains [1][8].

### Structural Plasticity
Structural plasticity involves physical alterations in brain anatomy. Key processes include dendritic spine formation, axonal sprouting, synaptogenesis, and the proliferation of neuroglial cells [2][8]. **Adult neurogenesis**—the generation of new neurons—remains a focal point of debate. While robustly documented in rodents and birds, conclusive histological evidence for functional neurogenesis in the adult human brain remains inconclusive, with proposed sites largely restricted to the hippocampal dentate gyrus and subventricular zone [7][8].

### Functional Plasticity
Functional plasticity refers to the brain's ability to alter how neurons communicate and process information without necessarily changing gross anatomy. Primary mechanisms include:
- **Synaptic Plasticity:** Experience-dependent, long-lasting changes in synaptic strength, primarily governed by **Long-Term Potentiation (LTP)** and **Long-Term Depression (LTD)** [3][7]. LTP, heavily reliant on NMDA receptor activation, calcium influx, and subsequent gene expression changes, serves as the primary cellular model for memory encoding and learning [11][13].
- **Functional Reorganization:** Following injury, undamaged brain regions may assume lost functions through **equipotentiality** (compensation by homologous contralateral areas) or **vicariation** (unrelated cortical areas taking over new functions) [7]. Conversely, **diaschisis** describes functional deficits in distant, anatomically connected brain regions triggered by localized damage, such as thalamic hypoperfusion following a middle cerebral artery stroke [7].
- **Cross-Modal Plasticity:** Sensory deprivation (e.g., congenital blindness or deafness) can trigger the repurposing of dormant cortical areas to process alternative sensory inputs, often enhancing residual modalities like peripheral visual attention or tactile sensitivity [8].

## Clinical and Behavioral Applications
The principles of neuroplasticity form the scientific foundation for modern neurorehabilitation, therapeutic interventions, and cognitive enhancement strategies [2][7].

- **Stroke and Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) Recovery:** Post-injury plasticity unfolds in distinct phases: acute neuronal loss and network disruption, subacute synaptic remodeling with a shift from inhibitory to excitatory states, and chronic axonal sprouting and network stabilization [3][7]. Rehabilitation modalities such as Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy (CIMT), mirror therapy, and task-specific repetitive training leverage these mechanisms to guide adaptive rewiring and restore motor/cognitive functions [3][7].
- **Skill Acquisition and Learning:** Learning complex skills (e.g., musical instruments, navigation, or motor sequences) induces both transient homeostatic responses and sustained structural remodeling in relevant cortical networks, including the prefrontal cortex, cerebellum, and basal ganglia [8][14].
- **Psychiatric and Neurological Disorders:** Emerging pharmacological approaches utilize plasticity mechanisms to treat depression via rapid-acting "psychoplastogens" (e.g., ketamine) that restore dendritic spine density and functional connectivity [8]. In ADHD, stimulant medications and cognitive training normalize fronto-parietal and striatal circuits, while addiction recovery focuses on weakening maladaptive reward pathways in the nucleus accumbens through behavioral substitution and neurofeedback [2][8]. Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, including repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), are increasingly used to modulate cortical excitability and accelerate therapeutic plasticity [3][7].

## Modulating Factors and Lifestyle Interventions
Neuroplasticity is highly sensitive to physiological, environmental, and behavioral variables. Factors that promote adaptive plasticity include:
- **Physical Exercise:** Aerobic activity upregulates neurotrophic factors such as BDNF, IGF-1, and VEGF, which cross the blood-brain barrier to enhance hippocampal neurogenesis, synaptic connectivity, and prefrontal gray matter volume [2][8].
- **Sleep & Metabolic Health:** Sleep facilitates synaptic consolidation, memory stabilization, and homeostatic downscaling. Intermittent fasting and specific dietary patterns have also been shown to promote neuronal growth and adaptive synaptic responses [2][7].
- **Cognitive & Social Engagement:** Novel experiences, multilingualism, meditation, artistic training, and music therapy consistently correlate with increased gray/white matter density, enhanced cognitive flexibility, and preserved neural connectivity during aging [2][8].

Conversely, aging, chronic psychological stress, sleep deprivation, and neurodegenerative pathologies degrade neuromodulator availability (e.g., dopamine, serotonin) and synaptic efficacy, thereby constraining plastic potential [3][7].

## Maladaptive Neuroplasticity
While typically beneficial, neuroplasticity can drive pathological outcomes when reorganization reinforces dysfunctional circuits. This "maladaptive plasticity" underlies conditions such as phantom limb pain, chronic pain syndromes (via central sensitization and cortical somatotopic distortion), use-dependent dystonia, and addiction-related reward pathway entrenchment [3][7][8]. In these contexts, therapeutic strategies focus on disrupting aberrant synaptic weights, normalizing neuroinflammation, and redirecting network activity toward healthier functional states through targeted rehabilitation or pharmacotherapy [2][7].

## Recent Advances and Methodological Shifts
Contemporary research is transitioning from descriptive neuroimaging to mechanistic, cellular-level resolution. A 2026 study published in *PLOS Biology* utilized ultra-high-gradient diffusion MRI combined with the Soma and Neurite Density Imaging (SANDI) model to disentangle transient versus sustained plasticity during motor sequence learning [12]. The findings revealed that learning triggers a short-lived, homeostatic swelling of cell bodies across engaged networks, alongside a regionally specific, lasting increase in cell-process density compatible with true structural remodeling [12]. This compartment-specific approach bridges historical gaps between animal models and living human brains, offering precise biomarkers for tracking therapeutic efficacy and cognitive development.

## Research Gaps and Contradictions
Despite extensive literature, several contradictions and methodological limitations persist:
- **Adult Neurogenesis in Humans:** While robust in non-human mammals, conclusive histological evidence for functional neurogenesis in the adult human brain remains elusive. Biomarker specificity and the identification of true neurogenic niches in human tissue continue to pose challenges [7][8].
- **Brain Training Efficacy:** Commercial cognitive training programs and "neuroplasticity games" often demonstrate task-specific improvements but lack reliable generalization to real-world executive functions, sparking debate over whether observed changes reflect genuine broad plasticity or localized skill acquisition [2][8].
- **Causality vs. Correlation:** Many neuroimaging studies demonstrate structural or functional changes alongside behavioral improvements, but establishing direct causal pathways between specific synaptic events and macroscopic cognitive gains remains methodologically complex [12].
- **Theoretical Fragmentation:** As noted by multiple researchers, neuroplasticity is frequently used as an umbrella term without a unified theoretical framework, complicating cross-disciplinary synthesis and standardized measurement [1][8].
